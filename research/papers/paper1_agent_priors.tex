\title{Transferable Operational Failure Priors for Coding Agents}

\author{Physis Research Program}
\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
Coding agents fail in predictable ways, and we ask whether that
predictability transfers between agents. Using a corpus of 75,933 actions
from four harnesses (Claude Code, Codex, Cline, OpenCode) across three
machines, we test whether action-kind failure rates learned from one agent
predict another agent's failures. Twelve directional source$\to$target
comparisons are evaluated by session-level bootstrap --- the correct
inferential unit, since actions cluster within sessions --- with
family-wise correction over the twelve tests (Holm). \textbf{No pair shows
positive transfer.} The only three comparisons to survive Holm are
significantly \emph{below} chance (AUC 0.394, 0.401, 0.416), i.e.\ the
borrowed prior misleads rather than merely failing to help. The single
pair that appeared to transfer in our earlier analysis,
cline$\to$opencode (0.534, raw $p = 0.009$), does not survive Holm; it
would need $p \le 0.006$. Under the less conservative
Benjamini--Hochberg procedure it does survive, as one of six, the other
five being anti-predictive --- so we report the positive result as
BH-dependent and not established. We also show that the choice of
bootstrap unit is a first-class methodological variable here: the
incorrect action-level bootstrap makes two pairs look like positive
transfer where the correct session-level unit finds none. On the positive
side, we can report one leak-free result: our earlier \emph{cold-start} claim
was contaminated by train/test leakage and has been withdrawn and re-run. In
the corrected experiment --- priors built only on a held-out training pool,
scored on a fixed held-out test set, paired intervals --- the borrowed prior
sits \emph{below} chance at every history size (0.483) and never contributes,
while two training sessions of local history already reach 0.600 and beat the
borrowed prior by $+0.114$. There is no regime in which borrowing helps,
which is a stronger claim than the one we originally made. We report the
negative result, and the anti-predictive result, as the primary findings.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large language model-based coding agents (Claude Code, Codex, Cline,
OpenCode) execute hundreds of tool calls per session. A small fraction of
these calls fail. Failure is not uniformly distributed across action kinds:
some (e.g., \texttt{bash:cd}, \texttt{read}) almost never fail, while
others (e.g., \texttt{bash:ssh}, \texttt{exec}) fail at rates an order of
magnitude higher. This structure is \emph{operational}: it belongs to the
agent's interaction with its environment, not to the code being written.

We ask: can a failure prior learned from one agent predict another agent's
failures? This is a \emph{transfer} question. The practical motivation is
cold-start: a new agent on a new machine has no local history, and a
borrowed prior from a different agent's accumulated record may be better
than nothing.

The question has a clear failure mode. If failure structure is primarily a
property of the user, the repository, or the task, then cross-agent transfer
will be at chance. If it is a property of the agent's tool-use patterns,
then transfer will succeed wherever the two agents' action-kind vocabularies
overlap.

\section{Data}

We use a redacted corpus of 75,933 coding-agent actions extracted from
402 sessions across three machines (\texttt{pansa}, \texttt{omo},
\texttt{feather}) and four harnesses (Table~\ref{tab:corpus}). Targets

\section{Method}

For each harness $h$, we compute per-action-kind failure rates:
\[
\hat{p}_h(k) = \frac{\text{failures of kind } k \text{ in } h}{\text{total actions of kind } k \text{ in } h}.
\]

For cross-harness transfer from source $s$ to target $t$, we predict each
target action's failure probability using the source's rates:
\[
\hat{p}(a) = \begin{cases}
\hat{p}_s(\text{kind}(a)) & \text{if kind}(a) \in \text{source vocabulary} \\
0.5 & \text{otherwise (chance for unseen kinds).}
\end{cases}
\]

We score predictions against target $t$'s labels using the area under the
receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). A bootstrap over target
sessions (500 resamples) provides confidence intervals.

For the in-corpus baseline, we use leave-one-session-out: fit
$\hat{p}_h(k)$ on all sessions but one, predict the held-out session.

\textbf{The inferential unit.} All intervals below bootstrap over
\emph{sessions}, not actions. Actions are clustered within sessions
(median 101 actions per session), so an action-level bootstrap treats
correlated observations as independent and narrows the interval
artificially. The effect is smaller than we first assumed but real: under
an action-level bootstrap two of twelve pairs have intervals excluding
chance, while under the session-level bootstrap one does. The pair that
changes status is opencode$\to$cline, which moves from 0.519 [0.505, 0.534]
to 0.519 [0.468, 0.573] --- the point estimate is identical and only the
interval width changes, which is precisely the failure mode. We report
session-level intervals throughout, and the recomputation is stored in
\texttt{research/bootstrap\_unit\_check.json}.

\subsection{Multiplicity correction}
\label{sec:multiplicity}

Twelve directional tests are reported. Selecting the smallest $p$ from twelve
and quoting its interval is not evidence of an effect; the interval does not
account for the selection. We therefore apply family-wise correction over the
twelve comparisons, on session-level bootstrap $p$-values from 10{,}000
replicates.

\textbf{No pair shows positive transfer after family-wise correction.} The
three comparisons that survive Holm are all significantly \emph{below} chance
(0.394, 0.401, 0.416) --- the borrowed prior is anti-predictive, not merely
uninformative. All three involve Codex, as source in two cases and target in
one; we do not have a mechanism for that and flag it as an observation, not a
finding.

The pair that appeared to transfer in our earlier analysis,
cline$\to$opencode (0.534), is \emph{nominally} significant only. Its Holm
threshold is set by its rank among the twelve: it sits at rank 5, so the
denominator is $m - r + 1 = 12 - 5 + 1 = 8$ and the threshold is
$0.05/8 = 0.00625$. Its nominal $p = 0.0094$ exceeds that, so it does
\emph{not} survive Holm. We use ``nominally significant'' for unadjusted
$p < 0.05$ throughout and reserve ``survives Holm'' for corrected results;
six pairs are nominally significant and three survive Holm. We therefore report it as
\emph{BH-dependent}: it is the only positive-direction comparison in the
family, it is the weakest of the six BH rejections, and the more conservative
procedure that controls the probability of \emph{any} false rejection does not
retain it.

Five of the twelve pairs are significantly below chance under the session-level
unit (six under the action-level unit), which reframes the headline. ``Cross-
agent failure priors do not transfer'' understates the result: where this
corpus detects a cross-harness effect at all, the effect is usually that a
borrowed prior makes the target's failures \emph{less} predictable. The
significance of that anti-prediction is what survives correction; the single
positive result does not.

Stored in \texttt{research/e1\_multiplicity.json}; the Fisher combined test
over the twelve gives $p \approx 5\times10^{-10}$ but is optimistic here
because the comparisons share harnesses, so we do not rely on it.

Two further pairs have point estimates above 0.5 but intervals that
include it, and are therefore not established:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{opencode$\to$cline} (0.519, 95\% CI [0.468, 0.573]):
  74.3\% of cline's action kinds appear in opencode's vocabulary, yet the
  interval spans chance.
\item \textbf{opencode$\to$codex} (0.503, 95\% CI [0.179, 0.555]):
  three target sessions give an interval too wide to interpret. This pair is
  the clearest case of the point-estimate/bootstrap-mean divergence flagged
  in Table~\ref{tab:multiplicity}: its point estimate is 0.503 but its
  bootstrap mean is 0.458, because the resampling distribution for a
  3-session target is heavily skewed. Where the two disagree, the bootstrap
  mean and the interval are the ones to read.
\end{itemize}

The weakest point estimate is \textbf{codex$\to$cline} (0.399, CI
[0.359, 0.448]) --- and with the multiplicity correction applied it is no
longer merely the weakest, it is one of the three comparisons that survive
Holm as significantly \emph{below} chance. Codex has only 3 sessions and 662
actions, so its per-kind estimates are sparse; sparse estimates that are
systematically wrong in one direction are exactly what produces a reliably
anti-predictive prior.

\subsection{In-corpus baselines}

In-corpus AUC (leave-one-session-out, marker\_error label, session bootstrap):
\begin{itemize}
\item claude-code: 0.627 [0.606, 0.656] (42 sessions, 14,457 actions)
\item cline: 0.751 [0.690, 0.806] (69 sessions, 12,354 actions)
\item codex: 0.461 [0.341, 0.508] (3 sessions, 662 actions)
\item opencode: 0.680 [0.642, 0.718] (288 sessions, 48,460 actions)
\end{itemize}

Codex's in-corpus AUC (0.461) is at chance, and its interval is by far the
widest: 3 sessions provide too little data for a per-kind prior. Two of the
three other harnesses' priors exceed Codex's own baseline on point estimate
(opencode$\to$codex: 0.503; cline$\to$codex: 0.513) while one does not
(claude-code$\to$codex: 0.442). All three intervals overlap Codex's own, so
none of these comparisons is individually established. Pooled, they look
like a \textbf{cold-start} effect --- a borrowed prior from a larger corpus
beating a thin local history --- but three overlapping intervals do not
establish it. This comparison is not affected by the leakage that
invalidated our original cold-start curve (codex's own baseline is
leave-one-session-out and the borrowed priors come from other harnesses, so
both arms are clean); it remains unestablished for the ordinary reason that
the intervals overlap. The corrected within-target experiment in
Section~\ref{sec:coldstart} is what speaks to cold-start directly, and its
answer is that borrowing never helps --- the borrowed prior is below chance
at every history size, and local data beats it as soon as there is any.

\subsection{Coverage correlates with transfer success}
\label{sec:coverage}

Figure~\ref{fig:coverage} plots transfer AUC against coverage --- the
fraction of target action kinds the source has observed. Coverage
correlates positively with transfer AUC (Pearson $r = 0.43$, $n = 12$,
$p > 0.05$), but the relationship is \textbf{not} a threshold. Two pairs
with coverage above 0.46 still fall below chance: claude-code$\to$cline
(coverage 0.645, AUC 0.472) and claude-code$\to$codex (coverage 0.470,
AUC 0.442). Conversely the best transfer in the matrix, cline$\to$opencode
(AUC 0.534), has coverage of only 0.375.

The pairs that fail worst (codex$\to$cline at 0.399, codex$\to$opencode at
0.415) have two of the lowest coverage values in the matrix (0.353 and
0.172), which invites the reading that low coverage predicts failure. The
matrix refutes that reading twice over. First, monotonicity fails within a
single source: codex$\to$claude-code has \emph{lower} coverage than
codex$\to$cline (0.241 against 0.353) yet a \emph{higher} AUC (0.489
against 0.399) --- moving coverage down moved performance up. Second, the
matrix's best transfer contradicts the ordering it would need:
cline$\to$opencode achieves the highest AUC in the matrix (0.534) at
coverage 0.375, only 0.022 above the failing codex$\to$cline pair, and the
two differ by 0.135 in AUC. Coverage therefore does not order these pairs,
and we do not claim sufficiency in either direction. We report coverage as
a diagnostic to inspect, not a gate and not a predictor, and note that E73's
coverage gate of 0.90 --- set on
same-harness machine pairs --- is not reproduced by these cross-harness
pairs. Because no pair shows positive transfer once the twelve comparisons
are corrected for multiplicity (Section~\ref{sec:multiplicity}), this
correlation rests on twelve point estimates of which eleven are
indistinguishable from chance and three are significantly below it; a
pattern in which the two worst pairs
also happen to have low coverage is the pattern expected by construction in
such a sample. We present the correlation as exploratory, do not interpret
it causally, and would require a larger harness set before treating it as a
scaling relationship.

\subsection{Cold-start: a withdrawn curve, and a corrected experiment}
\label{sec:coldstart}

\textbf{First, the withdrawal.} Our originally reported curve --- 0.469
(borrowed only) rising to 0.720 (own history), with a crossover at $n=2$ ---
is contaminated and is not a result. It was produced by building the local
prior from target sessions and then scoring that prior on \emph{the same}
target sessions: \texttt{own\_only} (n=42) used all 42 claude-code sessions
to build and all 42 to score, and \texttt{mixed\_n}$N$ scored on all of
\texttt{tgt} including the \texttt{tgt[:N]} it was built from. The
\texttt{transferred\_only} point was the only uncontaminated one.

\emph{As originally reported (withdrawn, retained as an error record):}
0.469 [0.445, 0.490] for the borrowed prior alone, 0.720 [0.700, 0.743] for
own history, mixed exceeding borrowed-only at $n=2$ (0.598 [0.562, 0.635]).

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{fig2_cold_start_curve.pdf}
\caption{WITHDRAWN --- shown only to document the error. This is the original
cold-start curve. The \texttt{own\_only} and \texttt{mixed\_n}$N$ points
built their priors from sessions contained in the test set, so their apparent
advantage over the borrowed-only point is leakage, not local history. Do not
read any part of it as a result; Table~\ref{tab:coldstart} supersedes it.}
\label{fig:coldstart}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Second, the corrected experiment.} We re-ran it with the target
harness's 42 sessions split once, seeded, into a 30-session training pool and
a 12-session held-out test set (6{,}132 test actions). Every arm is scored on
the same held-out set; $N$ is swept over the training pool only, so no test
session ever contributes to a prior; and the comparison is paired --- one
resample of test sessions, both arms scored on it, difference recorded,
2{,}000 times --- so the interval is on the difference. Four arms: local-only
(first $N$ training sessions), borrowed-only (\texttt{opencode}, no target
data), mixed (local + borrowed counts), and pooled-others (all non-target
harnesses).

Three things follow, and the third overturns our original framing.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Local history pays off almost immediately.} Two training
  sessions are enough to reach 0.600, and by 20 sessions the local prior sits
  at 0.656.
\item \textbf{The borrowed prior is below chance at every $N$, and never
  improves.} It is fixed at 0.483 because it is fixed \emph{in fact} --- no
  amount of target data enters it. This is the same anti-predictive pattern
  Section~\ref{sec:multiplicity} finds, now visible within a single target.
\item \textbf{There is no cold-start regime in the sense we claimed.} Our
  original story was that a borrowed prior helps while local history is thin
  and loses once it matures. The corrected curve has no such region: the
  borrowed prior never helps, at any $N$, and local data beats it as soon as
  there is any. The claim we can defend is weaker in mechanism and stronger
  in force --- \emph{borrowed priors do not help even where we expected them
  to help most}.
\end{enumerate}

Mixed and pooled-others track local-only closely and never separate from it:
at $N=10$ local-only (0.652) is in fact \emph{above} mixed (0.629), and at
$N=2$ the difference is within noise. Adding borrowed counts to local data
buys nothing measurable here.

Stored in \texttt{research/e1\_coldstart\_paired.json}. This experiment is
what the original should have been: the defect was leakage, not the choice of
arms, and the leakage is what produced both the apparent improvement and the
apparent crossover.

\section{Discussion}

\textbf{What transfers.} Almost nothing transfers across harnesses, and the
exception does not survive correction. Only one of twelve pairs has a
point estimate above chance with an interval excluding it
(cline$\to$opencode, 0.534), and it fails Holm
(Section~\ref{sec:multiplicity}); it survives only the less conservative
Benjamini--Hochberg. Its mechanism, insofar as we can read one, is ordinary:
different harnesses share tool vocabularies (Bash, Read, Edit, Write, Grep),
and where two harnesses' kind distributions happen to align the per-kind
rate carries over. We cannot identify a property of a pair that predicts
which case it will be --- coverage, the most plausible candidate, is
exploratory at best on twelve points.

\textbf{What anti-transfers.} The reliable effect in this corpus runs the
other way. Three pairs are significantly \emph{below} chance after
family-wise correction (0.394, 0.401, 0.416), all involving Codex. Codex's
corpus is 3 sessions and 662 actions, so its per-kind rate estimates are
sparse; a sparse estimate that is systematically wrong in one direction
produces a prior that is reliably worse than guessing. We flag this as an
observation rather than a mechanism --- we have one small harness, not a
study of small harnesses --- but it is the only cross-harness effect here
that survives correction, and it is the result the title should be read
against: the danger is not that borrowed priors fail to help, it is that
they can mislead.

\textbf{What does not transfer.} Codex's 3-session corpus is too small for
its own in-corpus prior to be useful (AUC 0.461), and other harnesses'
priors beat it on point estimate. We previously read this as evidence of a
cold-start effect; that reading depended on a within-target curve which we
have since withdrawn for train/test leakage
(Section~\ref{sec:coldstart}), and the present comparison is unestablished
for the ordinary reason that the intervals overlap. Across all twelve
pairs the borrowed prior never reaches the target's own mature baseline.

\textbf{Label drift.} The four harnesses use different failure labels
(harness flags vs.\ real exit codes vs.\ output markers). This introduces
a small, uncontrolled confound into every cross-harness number. The
direction of the bias is unknown but likely conservative: label noise
attenuates AUC.

\textbf{Scope.} One user's fleet, one fortnight, four harnesses. The
``cross-agent'' label is four harnesses but one human. Cross-user
transfer --- whether a prior learned from one developer predicts another
developer's failures --- is untested and is the highest-priority external
replication.

\section{Conclusion}

Cross-agent operational failure priors do not transfer, and the effect this
corpus can detect is the opposite of the one we went looking for. Under
session-level bootstrap --- the correct inferential unit --- and family-wise
correction over the twelve comparisons, \emph{no} pair shows positive
transfer. The three pairs that survive Holm are significantly \emph{below}
chance (0.394, 0.401, 0.416): a prior borrowed from one harness makes
another harness's failures less predictable than chance would. The single
positive-direction result, cline$\to$opencode at 0.534, survives
Benjamini--Hochberg but not Holm, and we report it as BH-dependent rather
than established.

This is a stronger claim than ``priors do not transfer''. The literature
assumption worth testing is that failure statistics are a portable property
of a task domain; on this corpus, where they are detectable across harnesses
at all, they are actively misleading. That is a negative result with a
sign, and it is the one the data supports.

The within-target experiment agrees. Having withdrawn our original cold-start
curve for train/test leakage and re-run it properly --- priors built only on
a held-out training pool, all arms scored on a fixed held-out test set,
paired intervals --- the borrowed prior sits below chance at every history
size and never contributes, while two sessions of local history are already
worth $+0.114$ AUC over it. We originally hoped to find a regime where
borrowing is worth doing. There is none here: the answer to ``should I
borrow a failure prior from another agent?'' is no, at every amount of local
data from zero upward.

Three methodological points follow. First, the bootstrap unit is a
first-class variable in this kind of evaluation: the action-level bootstrap
reports two pairs as positive transfer where the session-level bootstrap
reports none, and the only thing that changes is interval width. Clustering
actions within sessions inflates apparent significance, and
agent-trajectory work should resample sessions. Second, a plausible and
often-assumed property of the data --- that vocabulary coverage governs
transfer --- is at best exploratory here, since it is estimated from
eleven chance-level point estimates. Third, and most transferable: the
original cold-start curve looked smooth, monotone and plausible, and it was
an artifact of scoring a prior on the sessions it was built from. A curve
that rises with the amount of data an arm is allowed to see should be
suspected of exactly that, and the check is cheap --- rebuild the prior from
a disjoint split and see whether the curve survives.

The honest summary is negative: failure structure is only weakly a
property of the agent as opposed to the user, the repository, and the task.
A single-user, single-fortnight corpus cannot separate those, and our
cross-harness comparisons mostly cannot either.

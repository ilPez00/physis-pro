However, logical reasoning that ontologies can directly support are quite limited in learning, approximation and prediction. One straightforward solution is to integrate statistical analysis and machine learning. To this end, automatically learning vector representation for knowledge of an ontology i.e., ontology embedding has been widely investigated in recent years. Numerous papers have been published on ontology embedding, but a lack of systematic reviews hinders researchers from gaining a comprehensive understanding of this field. To bridge this gap, we write this survey paper, which first introduces different kinds of semantics of ontologies, and formally defines ontology embedding from the perspectives of both mathematics and machine learning, as well as its property of faithfulness. I Introduction

Ontologies are formal, explicit and shared representations of knowledge within a domain, with definitions and axioms for concepts, properties, relations, and other types of entities [1]. Ontologies have been a critical technology in Knowledge Management, Information Systems, the Semantic Web, Natural Language Processing and Artificial Intelligence, playing an increasingly important role in many fields such as Healthcare, Bioinformatics and E-commerce. A simple ontology can just be a set of concepts arranged in a hierarchy with the subsumption (inclusion) relationship between two concepts for indicating that instances of one concept all belong to another. Such ontologies are capable of representing taxonomies of domains, such as the BBC Widelife Ontology [2] and the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) [3], dating back to Porphyrian Tree for presenting Aristotle’s categories in the third century AD. Meanwhile, many data and knowledge management systems such as the product databases of e-commerce platforms [4] also adopt such simple ontologies for the type information of data.

With the fast development of the Web in 1990s, representing and exchanging data and knowledge on the Web became desirable. To this end, several standards were proposed for defining more complex ontologies for constructing the Semantic Web [5][6]. In 1999, Resource Description Framework (RDF)1 which defines the syntax of triple (Subject, Predicate, Object) was proposed for representing data, and in 2000, the vocabulary of RDF Schema (RDFS)2, was proposed for building ontologies as the schemas of data [7]. The vocabulary of RDFS can represent not only hierarchical concepts, but also instance membership to concepts, property hierarchies, and property domains and ranges.

The Web Ontology Language (OWL), including its second version OWL 2, was published upon the foundation of RDF and RDFS for building ontologies that can represent more complex knowledge with logics such as the disjunction, conjunction and disjointness of concepts, and the existential and universal rules3. OWL was underpinned by Description Logic — a fragment of first-order logic with decidable reasoning and efficient decision procedures [8]. Many widely used ontologies such as the Gene Ontology (GO) [9], the Food Ontology (FoodOn) [10], the DBpedia ontology [11] and the aforementioned BBC Widelife Ontology all adopt OWL [12].

Besides the formally and explicitly defined semantics, most real-world ontologies are also equipped with literals, including attribute values of instances defined by data properties, and meta-data associated with entities4 defined by annotation properties5, representing information of name, definition, comment, image, versions, provenance and so on. For example, the concept obo:FOODON_00002873 in FoodOn is associated with an English name “okara”, a synonym “soy pulp”, a long definition “Okara, soy pulp, or tofu dregs is a pulp consisting of insoluble parts of the soybean that remains after pureed soybeans are filtered …”, the source of this definition, and an image of okara [10]. These annotations, originally created for human understanding, contain important information that is often complementary to the formal semantics. However, they are cannot be utilised by symbolic reasoning.

In early 2010s, word embedding algorithms like Word2Vec were proposed to represent natural language words as low dimensional vectors with their semantics like correlations concerned in the vector space [13]. Similar ideas of representation learning were also applied to knowledge graphs (KGs) that are composed of relational facts, giving rise to some classic algorithms like RESCAL [14], TransE [15] and RDF2Vec [16]. The instances and relations (i.e., object properties) are embedded with their semantics indicated by the facts retained in the vector space. Take TransE as an example; the embedding of a KG, denoted as a mapping function 𝒗⁡(⋅) from its instances and relations to vectors, is learned such that each relational fact (h,r,t), where the head instance h, the relation r and the tail instance t correspond to the subject, predicate and object in an RDF triple, respectively, is kept in the vector space as 𝒗⁡(h)+𝒗⁡(r)≈𝒗⁡(t). Such vector representations not only enable the original symbolic knowledge to be consumed by machine learning and statistical algorithms, but also support neural-symbolic reasoning within a KG, with both approximation and prediction [17][18].

In the past ten years, similar representation learning techniques have also been applied to ontologies for learning their embeddings. Some early works, such as [19] and [20], proposed to embed triples with relations of transitivity, including those from WordNet [21]. Their methods are applicable to simple ontologies with concept hierarchies. However, the semantics of RDFS and OWL ontologies are much more complex, and, accordingly, more advanced vector representation models, which use high dimensional balls, boxes and axis-aligned cones for representing concepts, were recently proposed, following some early works including EmbedS [22] for RDFS ontologies, ELEmbeddings [23] and E2R [24] for OWL ontologies of Description Logic ℰ​ℒ+⁣+ and 𝒜​ℒ​𝒞, respectively. Meanwhile, a few complex embedding frameworks such as OPA2Vec [25] and OWL2Vec* [26] were also proposed to embed both formally defined semantics and textual literals, often upon sequence learning methods like word embeddings [13] and Transformer-based models [27].

Such ontology embedding methods have brought new solutions for ontology engineering and dramatically extended the application of ontologies. Many of them have been verified in tasks with real-world data, including concept subsumption inference [26], domain applications of protein–protein interaction prediction [25] and protein function prediction [28], ontology augmented zero-shot and few-shot learning [29][28]. In comparison with fuzzy ontology standards such as Fuzzy OWL [30], traditional ontology learning methods such as the inductive logic programming system DL-Leaner [31][32], and some neural-symbolic frameworks that can also combine ontologies with machine learning such as Logic Tensor Network [33], ontology embedding methods focus on the automatic learning of the given knowledge’s vector representations so as to supporting the integration with different machine learning and statistical models for different applications including both semantic reasoning within ontologies and consuming ontologies in other systems.

Briefly, there are quite a few results about ontology embeddings, covering theoretic analysis, new methods, and successful applications. Although some survey papers for KG embedding involve ontologies [34][35][36], but they only analyse those embedding methods that aim at relational facts, regarding simple ontologies as a kind of additional constraints. There is a shortage of systematic review to papers on ontology embeddings. It is worth mentioning that Kulmanov et al. [37] reviewed some works in 2021 on using machine learning for analyzing semantic similarity with ontologies. Several ontology embedding methods including Onto2Vec [38], OPA2Vec [25] and EL Embeddings [23] are covered, but they are far from complete, especially considering there are quite a few new papers published since 2021.

This survey aims to bridge the above gap, with (i) systematic categorisation and comparison of the ontology embedding methods according to the employed techniques and the target ontologies, (ii) review of the applications in supporting knowledge engineering, machine learning and life science knowledge discovery together with benchmarks and metrics, (iii) introduction and result demonstration of a library named mOWL [39] that can support the implementation of ontology embedding methods, and (iv) discussion on the challenges and future directions. It has reviewed over 80 papers (around 40 of them are for new embedding methods) published in conferences and journals of Computer Science, AI and Bioinformatics, covering all the relevant works on ontology embedding, to the best of our knowledge. We believe it will benefit all the researchers who are interested in some topics among ontology, KG, knowledge representation, semantic embedding, semantic techniques, knowledge engineering, neural-symbolic integration, bioinformatics, and AI for life sciences.

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. Section II gives the background of ontology and semantic embedding, including formal definitions and properties of ontology embedding. Section III and IV review ontology embedding methods, and their applications, respectively. Section V demonstrates mOWL. Section VI presents our perspectives on the challenges and future directions. Finally, Section VII concludes the paper.
II Background
II-A Symbolic Knowledge Representation with Ontologies
II-A1 Knowledge Graph (KG)

In this paper, we distinguish KGs from ontologies. We refer to KGs as those knowledge bases mainly composed of relational facts in RDF, following the definition in most KG embedding papers [40][17]. A KG can be denoted as 𝒢={𝑰,𝑹,𝑻}, where 𝑰 denotes a set of instances (which correspond to individuals in Description Logic and entities in many papers about KG), 𝑹 denotes a set of binary relations, and 𝑻 denotes a set of relational facts, i.e., 𝑻={(h,r,t)|h,t∈𝑰,r∈𝑹}, where (h,r,t) is an RDF triple, h and t, as the subject and object, are also called as the triple’s head and tail, respectively. One simple example is (Bob,hasFather,Alex). Sometimes (h,r,t) is also denoted in form of a relation r⁡(h,t). For real-world KGs of the Semantic Web, each instance or relation should be uniquely identified, usually by an Internationalized Resource Identifier (IRI).
II-A2 RDF Schema (RDFS)

RDFS can define either an ontological schema for a KG or an independent ontology with the following main features.

    •

    RDFS can define a set of concepts (classes) 𝑪 and assert that an instance belongs to a concept using the built-in predicate rdfs:type. For example, we can use the triple (Alex,rdfs:type,Father) to represent that A​l​e​x is an instance of Father.
    •

    RDFS can define the subsumption between concepts with the built-in predicate rdfs:subClassOf. Considering two concepts Father and Parent defined in 𝑪. The triple (Father,rdfs:subClsasOf,Parent) represents that Parent subsumes Father and Father is a sub-concept of Parent.
    •

    RDFS can define the domain and range of a relation (i.e., object property) with the built-in predicates of rdfs:domain and rdfs:range, respectively. If the domain (resp. range) of a relation r is a concept C, the heads (resp. tails) that are associated with r must be declared or inferred to be instances of C. For example, we can define the range of hasFather to Father, Parent and/or Male. RDFS can also define the range of a data property with built-in data types.
    •

    RDFS can define the subsumption between two properties with the built-in predicate rdfs:subPropertyOf, indicating that the instance pairs associated to one property all belong to those associated to another property. One example is (hasFather,rdfs:subPropertyOf,hasParent).

II-A3 Description Logic (DL) and Web Ontology Language (OWL)

OWL has different sub-languages and comes in multiple versions. The complete languages, OWL Full and OWL 2 Full, are not decidable. In this part we mainly introduce (i) the vocabularies of OWL 2 DL which are defined based on the DL fragment 𝒮​ℛ​𝒪​ℐ​𝒬, and (ii) some of its widely used sub-languages that are developed for different scenarios with a better balance between knowledge expressivity and reasoning complexity. A signature consists of three finite sets of symbols: a set 𝑵I of individual names, a set 𝑵C of concept names, and a set 𝑵R of role names. DL 𝒮​ℛ​𝒪​ℐ​𝒬 allows recursive concept definition as
		⊤|⊥|A|C⊓D|C⊔D|¬C|∃r.C|∀r.C| 	
		≥nr.C|≤nr.C|∃r.Self|{a} 	

where ⊤ is the top concept, ⊥ is the bottom concept, A∈𝑵C is an atomic (or named) concept, r∈𝑵R is an atomic role, a∈𝑵I is an individual, C and D are themselves (possibly complex) concepts, n is a number of cardinality, and Self indicates the concept itself [8]. We say a concept is complex when it is constructed with one or multiple logical operators such as ⊓, ⊔, ∃, ∀ and ¬. A DL ontology 𝒪 can be composed of a TBox 𝒯 and an ABox 𝒜. The TBox defines logical background knowledge in the form of concept subsumption axioms C⊑D (Generalized Concept Inclusion, GCI), and role axioms for logical background knowledge of role composition, role subsumption, and role characteristics like functionality, transitivity and so on. Sometimes these role axioms are separately divided into an RBox, and accordingly the DL ontology is composed of a TBox, an RBox and an ABox. The ABox contains concrete data including concept assertions in form of C⁡(a), and role assertions in form of r⁡(a,b). With the defined logic, symbolic reasoners can be applied to infer hidden knowledge (i.e., entailment reasoning), check the ontology consistency and find justification that leads to inconsistency.

𝒜​ℒ​𝒞 and ℰ​ℒ+⁣+ are two important fragments of DL that are widely investigated in ontology embedding. 𝒜​ℒ​𝒞 is known as Attributive Concept Language with Complements and allows recursive concept definition with ⊤|⊥|A|C⊓D|C⊔D|¬C|∃r.C|∀r.C [8]; 𝒜​ℒ​𝒞 is a prototypical DL mainly studied because it has most of the major features.

The DL fragment ℰ​ℒ+⁣+ which corresponds to OWL 2 EL profile allows recursive concept definition with ⊤|⊥|A|C⊓D|∃r.C|{a} [41]. Due to a high knowledge representation capability but a polynomial time complexity in reasoning, DL ℰ​ℒ+⁣+ is widely used by many real-world ontologies such as SNOMED CT [42]. II-A4 Ontology Literals

The literals of an ontology are mainly defined in two approaches. (1) The first approach is to associate instances with literals by datatype properties. These data properties are sometimes known as attributes and their values can be of different types such as natural language phrases and long text, real values, data and time, category, image and domain specific sequence (e.g., gene sequence). For example, an instance of Person may have attributes of address, height, birth data, avatar and so on. (2) The second approach is to associate entities with meta information by annotation properties. In real-world ontologies, the most common literals by annotation properties are usually in form of text. For example, in the ontology fragment in Figure 1, the concepts and properties are annotated with names by the built-in vocabulary of rdfs:label6, and obo:FOODON_00002809 is annotated with two long sentences — a comment by rdfs:comment and a definition by the ad-hoc annotation property obo:IAO_0000115. Some other literals such as links to relevant external sources, editors and images are also widely used. Although all these literals include important information, as informal semantics, they are often ambiguous and cannot be used for inference by symbolic ontology reasoners. In ontology embedding, ontologies either with or without literals are considered.
II-A5 Target Ontologies

In our context of ontology embedding, “ontology” refers either to a TBox, or any DL ontology with a non-empty TBox, i.e., (T​B​o​x,A​B​o​x,R​B​o​x) or (T​B​o​x,A​B​o​x) with T​B​o​x≠∅. Different embedding methods aim at different semantics of an ontology. Accordingly, we divide the ontologies considered in the current ontology embedding works into four kinds:

    •

    Simple Ontology which refers to DL ontologies that have only a TBox composed of subsumption axioms of named classes.
    •

    Complex Ontology which refers to DL ontologies that have a TBox composed of more than subsumption axioms of named classes, and an ABox in option.
    •

    Ontology with Literals which refers to simple or complex ontologies that have literals.
    •

    Ontology with KG which refers to KGs composed of large scale relational facts of multiple relations, equipped with DL ontologies that has a TBox as the KG’s schema.

II-B Semantic Embedding

In this part, we first summarise word embedding and KG embedding, both of which have highly motivated the research of ontology embedding, and then give formal definitions and properties of ontology embedding.
II-B1 Word Embedding

Word embedding algorithms such as Word2Vec [13] and Glove [43] learn vector representations of tokens (which are either words or sub-words) from a large corpus concerning their semantics such as co-occurrence in the sentences. Taking Word2Vec as an example, it learns a Feed-Forward Neural Network from natural language sentences by one of the two auto-encoding architectures — continuous skip-gram which predicts the surrounding tokens of each token and continuous Bag-of-Words which predicts a token based on its surrounding tokens. For each token, the hidden layer output of the network is its embedding. Tokens with more similar meanings or tokens with higher co-occurrence in the corpus are often closer in the vector space, which can support similarity-based retrieval and feature learning for text classification. Such embedded semantics can also partially support analogical reasoning, e.g., 𝒗⁡(k​i​n​g)−𝒗⁡(f​a​t​h​e​r)≈𝒗⁡(q​u​e​e​n)−𝒗⁡(m​o​t​h​e​r) where v⁡(⋅) denotes a token’s embedding.

The word embeddings by Word2Vec and Glove are non-contextual, which means each token in the corpus has one unique vector representation no matter what surrounding tokens it has. Recently, with the development of Transformer-based encoder architectures [27] and Pre-trained Language Models (PLMs) like BERT [44], contextual word embeddings have been widely developed and adopted. Taking BERT as an example, it learns a Transformer architecture from a corpus by predicting the masked token in each sentence and the next sentence of a given sentence. The vector representation of a token is based on the attentions from itself and its surrounding tokens of the sentence where the token lies. Given a sentence “the bank robber was seen on the river bank”, the first “bank” and the second “bank” have different vectors due to different surrounding tokens. Such contextual word embeddings encode more semantics and often perform better than non-contextual word embeddings in many tasks.

Besides natural language text, the above contextual and non-contextual word embedding techniques are also applicable to other kinds of sequential data, such as BioVec for biological sequences like genes and proteins [45], and Node2Vec for random walks (i.e., paths) from graphs [46]. Their great success in many domains especially NLP also motivate researchers to applying them to KGs and ontologies. For simplicity, in this paper we call word embedding as well as representation learning for other sequential data as sequence learning.
II-B2 Knowledge Graph Embedding

These methods learn vector representations of instances and relations from the relational facts in a KG with their semantics retained in the vector space [17]. In general, typical technical solutions can be divided into geometric modeling such as the translational method TransE [15], tensor decomposition such as the bilinear method DistMult [47], neural network modeling such as the Convolutional Neural Network-based method ConvE [48], random walk-based sequence modeling such as RDF2Vec [16]. TransE has been introduced in Section I. Taking RDF2Vec as another example, it first conducts randomly walk for extracting paths composed of instances and relations, and then learns a Word2Vec model for encoding them. KG embedding has also been extended to encode semantics beyond relational facts, especially textual literals in combination with word embedding methods [49][50], and logics such as horn rules, schemas and constraints with additional modeling methods [34][36].
II-B3 Definitions and Properties of Ontology Embedding

Although the notion of an embedding is widely used in machine learning and in the context of KGs, we find it useful to explicitly make our understanding of an embedding clear here as it will provide a guide for classifying different ontology embedding methods. We start with the definition of “embedding” as it is used in mathematics:
Definition 1 (Embedding (mathematics))

An embedding is an injective and structure-preserving map between two mathematical structures which can be algebraic, topological, or geometrical structures.

In machine learning, embedding is used in a somewhat different sense as any learned representation of some data item:
Definition 2 (Embedding (machine learning))

An embedding e is a learned representation of the elements of a mathematical structure.

The notion of “embedding” in machine learning is therefore related to, but less strict than, a structure-preserving map, and embeddings are representations learned from data, largely built upon the foundation of representation learning [51]. “Learning” here needs to be understood with respect to an optimization objective that is optimized through a learning process. In machine learning, and in particular when generating ontology embeddings, the learned representations are usually not arbitrary but rather aim to preserve some “semantics” of the original data. We capture this by defining the notion of “faithfulness” of an embedding:
Definition 3 (Faithfulness of embedding (machine learning))

An embedding (machine learning) e is “faithful” to an embedding (mathematics) f if e converges to f, and if the learning process that generates e achieves optimality, e is an embedding (mathematics).

With this understanding, the notion of “faithfulness” for embeddings requires specifying the mathematical structure to which the embeddings are faithful. There are at least three mathematical structures that can be assigned to ontologies: (1) the formal language, including its deductive calculus; (2) each model of the ontology, i.e., each of the model structures in which all ontology axioms are true; and (3) the class of all models of the ontology. We do not define these here explicitly, but each are defined elsewhere [52]. However, the syntactic structure of an ontology consists of a set of logical symbols (connectives, quantifiers, etc.), a set of non-logical symbols (constants, functions, relations), a set of well-formed formulas constructed from the logical and non-logical symbols, a set of axioms (a subset of the well-formed formulas), a set of inference rules for deriving new formulas from the axioms and previously derived formulas. The model structure over a signature (consisting of concept, individual, and relation symbols) consists of a non-empty set D, called the domain or universe of the model, an interpretation function I that assigns each individual symbol to an element of D, each concept symbol to a subset of D, and each binary relation symbol to a binary relation on D (i.e., a subset of D2). The semantic structure of an ontology 𝒪, M​o​d​(𝒪), consists of the collection of all structures that satisfy the axioms in 𝒪. M​o​d​(𝒪) can be seen as a class or a category, depending on the context. As a class, it is a collection of model structures. As a category, the objects are the model structures and the morphisms are the homomorphisms between the models that preserve the structure.

We can now define ontology embedding formally:
Definition 4 (Ontology embedding)

An ontology embedding is an embedding (machine learning) of the non-logical symbols (concept, relation, individual symbols) of an ontology 𝒪.

Ontology embeddings can be faithful if they approximate a mathematical structure induced by the ontology 𝒪, i.e., if they approximate an embedding (mathematics) of 𝒪:
Definition 5 (Faithful ontology embedding)

An embedding (machine learning) f of an ontology 𝒪 is faithful to the embedding (mathematics) e of 𝒪 if f converges to e. An embedding (machine learning) f of ontology 𝒪 is faithful to the mathematical structure 𝒮 of 𝒪 if f converges to an embedding (mathematics) e and e is an embedding of 𝒮.

Using this definition of faithfulness, the property of faithfulness of an ontology embedding model requires specifying the mathematical structure that the embedding aims to preserve. This structure can also be used to classify and distinguish ontology embedding models.
III Ontology Embedding Methods

In this section, we first analyse the main technical solutions that have been commonly adopted for ontology embedding (Section III-A), and then review the ontology embedding works for each kind of ontologies (Section III-B to III-E).
III-A General Technical Solutions

The technical solutions that are commonly adopted by the current ontology embedding methods include the following:

    •

    Geometric Modeling aims to generate embeddings that are faithful to some model structures of an ontology; they generate (or approximate) logical models of an ontology by interpreting concepts as geometric regions, interpret individuals as members of these regions, and relations as pairs of points standing in some geometric relation. Take the embedding algorithms ELBE [53] and Box2​EL [54] for the DL ℰ​ℒ+⁣+ as an example. They model an instance as a high dimensional point, i.e., one single vector, and models a concept as a high dimensional axis-aligned box represented by one vector for the box center and one vector for the box offset. The instantiation relation between an instance and a concept is modeled as the instance’s point lying within the concept’s box. The basic idea is demonstrated in Figure 2 with a toy example.
    •

    Sequence Modeling first transforms the ontology axioms and literals into sequences, and then adopts a sequence learning algorithm to learn entity embeddings from the sequences. The basic idea is demonstrated in Figure 3. Take the OWL ontology embedding method OWL2Vec∗ [26] as an example. It first extracts sequences composed of entities and textual literals from an OWL ontology by mapping the ontology to a graph and performing multiple random walks over the graph, and then learns a word embedding model from the sequences for encoding the entities and tokens in the text. These methods are usually partially faithful to correlations and co-occurrences of symbols in the axioms of the ontology.
    •

    Graph Propagation represents an ontology by a (multi-relation) graph with initial node representations, and then learns a graph propagation model for new node representations. The basic idea is demonstrated in Figure 3 together with sequence modeling. For example, for concept matching, the work [55] uses two Graph Convolutional Networks to learn embeddings of two cross-ontology concepts, respectively, by propagating the initial word embeddings of their surrounding concepts.

III-B Embedding Simple Ontology

The class hierarchies (or taxonomy) of an ontology imply its basic structural semantics. We refer to ontology embedding that solely consider class hierarchies as simple ontology embedding, as it omits more complex logical semantics beyond concept subsumption. {forest}
Fig. 4: Dimensions, their values and corresponding works of embedding simple ontology.

Geometric modeling is the most prevalent technique for simple ontology embedding. This includes box embedding in Euclidean space, hyperbolic distance-based embedding in hyperbolic space, cone embedding for both geometries, and graph propagation in hyperbolic space.

In the context of Euclidean space, a key approach is to devise a function that preserves the hierarchical order of entities. A typical work by [56] proposes using the reversed product order, essentially forming a Euclidean cone where each entity’s embedding value is at least that of its parent entity. Building on this, [57] extends to encode entities using probability densities instead of points. The box embedding is another typical construction which simulates the hierarchical ordering with hyper-rectangles, where a child entity’s box is consumed within its parent entity’s box. Unlike cone embeddings, which are typically parameterized by their apex, box embeddings require two vectors representing the minimal and maximal coordinates in the hyper-rectangle. To further improve box embeddings, [58] explores a probabilistic relaxation to achieve a smoother distribution, [59] investigates encoding dual hierarchical relationships (hypernym and meronym) at the same time, and [60] addresses the local identifiability issue, proposing the Gumbel-box process as a solution.

Hyperbolic space, with its expansive property and theoretical underpinning, is particularly suitable for representing hierarchical structures. The Poincaré embedding, as discussed by [19], is a typical approach that minimizes hyperbolic distances between related entities while maximizing the separation from unrelated ones in a unit Poincaré ball. This spatial arrangement places more general entities near the origin and more specific entities closer to the boundary, reflecting their hierarchical depth. However, numerical instabilities near the boundary of the manifold are a known challenge. To address this, [93] investigates an alternative hyperbolic model, the Lorenz (or Hyperboloid) model, while [64] proposes the extended Poincaré ball with geometric distortion. To augment the embedded semantics, [62] and [63] explores the integration of Poincaré embeddings with pre-trained word embeddings, while [91] utilises the hyperbolic graph convolutional networks (HGCN) [94] for aggregating neighbourhood information. In hyperbolic space, establishing geometric shapes like boxes and cones, which are straightforward in Euclidean space, requires more nuanced considerations. A notable contribution in this line is the hyperbolic entailment cone by [61], which not only preserves transitivity but also enables direct prediction of entity subsumptions through its construction.

The techniques discussed here aim to achieve high geometric interpretability in encoding hierarchies, whether preserving order or exploiting geometric properties. Although not all cited works specifically test with ontology class hierarchies, their methodologies are readily applicable to such structures.
III-C Embedding Complex Ontology
{forest}
Fig. 5: Dimensions, their values and corresponding works of embedding complex ontology.

Complex ontologies are mainly embedded by the solution of geometric modeling. The methods aim to map concepts (classes), individuals (instances) and roles (relations) into a continuous vector space (e.g., ℝn) where they can be represented as points or geometrical regions. This allows to capture some aspects of semantics of the underlying ontology by means of geometric properties of the embedding space and enables reasoning tasks such as subsumption inference. We provide an analysis of complex ontology embedding methods from four perspectives: embedding method, semantics complexity, strategy to embed ABox and RBox axioms, and theoretical analysis of the method; corresponding categorization and related works are shown in Figure 5.

Multiple geometric models have been developed recently for the lightweight DL ℰ​ℒ++. ELEmbeddings [23] and EmEL++ [67] represent named concepts as n-dimensional Euclidean balls. One of the main limitations of ball representation of classes is the impossibility to faithfully model their intersection since the intersection of two balls is no longer a ball in ℝn. To address this issue several methods based on boxes have been introduced [53][68][54]. They can be categorized further by the relation model implemented within their frameworks: ELBE [53] utilizes TransE [15]; this model based on vector translations cannot faithfully represent 1-to-N, N-to-N and N-to-1 relations. To overcome this limitation, Box2​EL [54] adapts the relational model of BoxE [96], which also allows for capturing role composition and role subsumption. BoxEL [68] develops an alternative way to elucidate relations by affine transformations which solves the problem of concept embedding size incompatibility. In [70] a proper non-convex geometric interpretation for concepts, roles and individuals is introduced first through mapping of interpretation domain elements into binary vectors, and then the convex hulls of constructed regions are considered. This method’s applicability to real-world applications remains an open question since it lacks implementation and empirical evaluation. [69] is another method that is mainly described from a theoretical perspective. It uses axis-aligned cones for concept interpretations in 𝒜​ℒ​𝒞 ontologies. As opposed to previously discussed works, this method builds partial models: as an example, if for some individual a only assertion axioms of (C⊔D)​(a) and C⁡(a) are presented within the ontology and neither D⁡(a) nor (¬D)​(a) can be proven, the embedding leverages multiple interpretations since there are several dissimilar ways to interpret a within this geometric framework. There are some other methods tailored to encode 𝒜​ℒ​𝒞 theories, using fuzzy sets [95] or ordered vector spaces over ℝn [71].

From the perspective of the expressivity of the DL embedded, many geometric models focus on encoding the constructs of ℰ​ℒ+⁣+. ELEmbeddings [23], ELBE [53] and BoxEL [68] work with the ℰ​ℒ fragment enriched with nominals omitting role inclusions. Subsumption axioms are interpreted as the containment of one geometric region within another one. EmEL++ [67] and Box2​EL [54] include objective functions for role inclusion and role chain axioms adopting geometrical containment for role interpretations: in Box2​EL, a box inclusion loss is used towards boxes that represent the head and tail parts of a role for role inclusion and chain axioms; in EmEL++ [67], the role hierarchy is interpreted via establishing partial order for vectors. [70] provides detailed information about each element in the interpretation domain (including to what individual it corresponds, in which classes it is contained and how it is related to other elements); while this approach can be applied to 𝒜​ℒ​𝒞, it requires to store sparse vectors of size |NI​(𝒪)|+|NC​(𝒪)|+|NR​(𝒪)|⋅|Δℐ| for each domain element where NI​(𝒪), NC​(𝒪), NR​(𝒪) are the sets of individual, concept, and role symbols, respectively, and Δℐ is the interpretation domain. Other methods under consideration [69][95][71] target the full expressivity of 𝒜​ℒ​𝒞; in particular, the negation of a concept is incorporated as a membership function of (¬C)ℐ assigned to all individuals via fuzzy negation operator applied to the corresponding membership function of Cℐ [95], by concept lattice enrichment [71], or using polar operators [69].

An ontology may have an ABox and an RBox, besides a TBox. Some embedding methods do not distinguish between individuals and concepts. They eliminate ABox and work solely with TBox axioms [23][53][71] by translating assertional axioms C⁡(a) and r⁡(a,b) into TBox axioms {a}⊑C and {a}⊑∃r.{b}, respectively. Those methods allowing for role chain and role inclusion [67][54] incorporate additionally RBox constructs. In the case that the ABox is retained, assertional axioms are either embedded alongside with terminological axioms [95][68][54] or placed into the latent space after the model of TBox is constructed [69]. In contrast with other approaches, the method in [70] does not explicitly represent the geometric interpretations of individuals, roles and concepts for ℰ​ℒ​ℋ ontologies: it constructs a binary vector μ⁡(d) of size |NI​(𝒪)|+|NC​(𝒪)|+|NR​(𝒪)|⋅|Δℐ| associated with each domain element d such that μ​(d)​[a]=1 if d=aℐ (for individuals), μ​(d)​[A]=1 if d∈Aℐ (for classes) and μ​(d)​[r,e]=1 if (d,e)∈rℐ (for relations) (μ​(d)​[a]=0, μ​(d)​[A]=0, μ​(d)​[r,e]=0 otherwise).

In order to show that learned embeddings construct a logical geometric model of a given ontology, many methods provide an explicit proof. In most cases, theoretical results state that if the optimization objective converges to a certain value during training and some other conditions are satisfied, then the theory has a model [54][23][95][68], and this model is constructed through the optimization process; in this sense, faithfulness (machine learning) holds. Sometimes, authors refer to this property as soundness [54][23][95]. For ℰ​ℒ​ℋ ontology embeddings [70] and axis-aligned cone embeddings [69] our definition of faithfulness is not applicable since in these works only interpretation domains and interpretation functions are introduced without describing optimization process which approximates geometric models. Definitions of strong and weak faithfulness discussed in these works are properties of an interpretation function ℐ.

As for geometric embeddings without theoretical proofs provided, EmEL++ [67] cannot faithfully capture the role hierarchy although the corresponding loss is present within the framework: the r⊑s loss is symmetric with respect to r and s, so r⊑s necessarily implies s⊑r; the same holds for role chains r1∘r2⊑s and r2∘r1⊑s. ELBE [53] has faithfulness property which relies on justifications discussed in [23] since each normal form loss in this framework is a multi-dimensional version of corresponding ELEmbeddings objectives; faithfulness (machine learning) in this particular case can be formulated as follows (using notations from the paper): if m​a​r​g​i​n is a vector with non-positive components and the total loss is equal to 0 then the model is constructed. CatE [71] does not generate models, yet it can be considered as a faithful method with respect to the lattice of ontology concept descriptions in the sense that the transformation from the ontology to the lattice is total and injective, and the embedding preserves lattice structure.

According to the analysis of these geometric models for complex ontology embedding, we have the following brief discussions:

    •

    Methods for complex ontology embedding vary from highly interpretable geometric models based on balls or axis-aligned boxes to fuzzy sets and order embeddings. They usually contribute in two aspects: supporting constructs that are not covered by previous models [67][54][69][95][70], and more fine-grained representation of an ontology [68][71][53].
    •

    In terms of expressivity, models for complex ontology embedding are tailored to main DL constructs of either ℰ​ℒ+⁣+ [23][67][53][54][68][70] or 𝒜​ℒ​𝒞 [69][95][71]. As one potential future direction, we can mention targeting the full expressivity of ℰ​ℒ+⁣+, motivated by real-world applications (e.g., many ontologies in biomedical domain) and stronger reasoning capabilities.
    •

    Applicability to real-world scenarios remains an open question for some embedding methods [69][70]. It would be beneficial to implement and evaluate them on real-world datasets for ontology completion.

III-D Embedding Ontology with Literals

We analyse those works for embedding ontologies with literals from four dimensions — embedding method, embedding type, ontology type and literal type. Their potential values as well as the corresponding works are shown in Figure 6. For the embedding type, we divide the methods into two kinds: general encoders which are separately trained and applicable to different downstream tasks, and coupled encoders which are jointly trained with some other attached models often for feature learning and cannot be applied without these models. For the literal type, we classify the works into those only use entity name information (usually in form of short phrases), and those use both entity name information and other literals such as long textual descriptions.
{forest}
Fig. 6: Dimensions, their values and corresponding works of embedding ontology with literals.

Most methods for embedding ontologies with literals adopt the solution of sequential modeling demonstrated in Figure 2. The biggest challenge of this solution lies in its first step — extracting literal-injected sequences from the ontology. Some methods such as ERSOM [80], DeepAlignment [82], 𝒩-ball Embeddings [83], SapBERT [86] and HiT [90] directly use the literals of an entity like names, descriptions and other texts as its sequences to learn its embedding. Such literal-alone sequences miss the formal semantics of the entity, and therefore, some other methods such as BERTSubs [88], SORBERT [89] and [84] extract context-augmented literal sequences by first exploring the serialisation of an entity’s contexts such as the axioms this entity is involved and its neighbourhood in a graph transformed from the ontology, and then textualisating the entity sequences by replacing (a part) of the entities by their names. For deeper integration of the literals and the formal semantics, some more complex strategies, such as merging corpora of different kinds of sequences, and concatenating a literal-alone sequence and a context-augmented literal sequence of an entity for a hybrid literal sequence, have also been proposed and implemented in OPA2Vec [25] and OWL2Vec∗ [26].

For the second step of the sequence learning solution, many methods prefer to directly train an encoder from the extracted sequences by unsupervised representation learning. ERSOM [80] trains a stacked auto-encoder which is a deep neural network with several hidden layers; DeepAlignment [82], OPA2Vec [25] and OWL2Vec∗ [26] directly trains a Word2Vec model with continuous skip-gram or CBOW; [84] trains a biomedical variant of BERT with masked token prediction and next sentence prediction. There are also some methods trying to utilize some external tasks and data for training the encoder. SORBERT [89] trains a sentence transformer with a siamese network architecture by minimizing the distance between matched concepts from two ontologies; SapBERT [86] trains a biomedical BERT by minimizing the distance between a mention from text and its matched entity in an ontology; HiT [90] re-trains variants of BERT by retaining the subsumption relationships of an ontology in a Poincaré ball. The encoders by the above two kinds of methods are usually quite general, i.e., they are applicable to different tasks and/or different other ontologies beyond the ones for training, and can be fed to different other models. On the other hand, some encoders for ontology embedding are trained in conjunction with some downstream models by specific tasks. BERTSubs [88] fine-tunes a BERT model and an attached classifier with pairs of concepts that have a subsumption relationship; OntoProtein [87] trains a BERT model in conjunction with learning the embeddings of proteins and the Gene Ontology. Such learned ontology embeddings usually have limited generality, and can only be applied together with model trained together.

Besides sequence learning, OntoZSL [29] embeds an RDFS ontology with entity names and descriptions by a geometric modeling method which extends TransE with additional losses between entity representations and literal representations. MEDTO [92] and [55] both use Graph Neural Networks to learn features of concepts via propagation over concept hierarchies of the ontology for minimizing distances between matched concepts. OPA2Vec [25] and OWL2Vec∗ [26] also use the solution of neural-symbolic integration by employing OWL reasoners for inferring hidden axioms for augmenting the sequence extraction.

With the above analysis on the current works, we have the following discussion for ontology embedding with literals.

    •

    Sequence modeling is the most widely used general solution to embed the ontology with literals. Among these works, using contextual word embedding techniques like Transformer-based PLMs often leads to embeddings with better performance in downstream tasks, but it is more challenging and requires more future efforts to train general encoders.
    •

    The solution of geometric modeling has been explored by only one work [29]. However, geometric modeling can train embeddings with higher interpretability, through which we can understand how knowledge are inferred and learn the impact of literals towards the formal semantics in the vector space (e.g., what additional semantics the literals bring, and what kind of formal semantics, i.e., axioms, they can be transformed into).
    •

    All most all the works in this part focus on textual literals such as short phrases of names and long text of descriptions. Literals of other types such as number are simply dealt with as test. More future efforts are worthy while to deal with the semantics of literals of different data types especially images for more comprehensive multi-modal ontology embedding.

III-E Embedding Ontology with KG

We analyse those works for embedding ontologies with KGs from two dimensions — embedding method and ontology type. Note we consider those works that use the KG for supporting ontology embedding or jointly embed the KG and the ontology, but ignore those works that only use the ontology as constraints or additional knowledge for supporting KG embedding (e.g., [98] and [99]). See [34] for a survey of the latter. As all the works under consideration use the technical solution of geometric modeling, we make a more fine-grained categorisation of them according to how the concepts are modeled. Their potential values as well as the corresponding works of each value are shown in Figure 7.
{forest}
Fig. 7: Dimensions, their values and corresponding works of embedding ontology with KG.

Some works consider joint embedding of a simple ontology with hierarchical concepts and a KG with relational facts [72][74][75][77]. TransC [72] represents each concept as a sphere modeled by a vector for its center and a real value for its radius, and presents an instance as a point modeled by a vector. The concept membership is then modeled by the inclusion of the instance’s point in the concept’s sphere, and the concept subsumption is modeled by the inclusion of their spheres. Their corresponding losses are jointly minimized together with the translation loss of TransE that models the relational fact. For higher expressivity, some more complex geometric objects are also used. TransEllipsoid [75] and EIKE [77] both model each concept as a high dimensional ellipsoid, and TransCuboid [75] models each concept as a high dimensional box. The ellipsoid and box are both modeled by one vector for the center and another vector for the boundary (i.e., offset). TransEllipsoid, TransCuboid and EIKE use similar losses as TransC for training. OntoEA [74] jointly embeds two KGs together with their ontologies into one vector space. It simply uses a point to represent each concept, and models not only concept subsumption, instance membership and relational facts, but also cross KG instance matching and concept disjointness.

The other works consider embedding of RDFS ontologies with KGs [22][73][76]. JOIE [73] transforms the RDFS ontology into an ontology view graph, with each concept modeled as a node, and each relation’s domain concepts and range concepts connected by a meta relation. In training, triples of the ontology view graph are equally modeled as normal KG triples by a translation loss, and the instance membership is modeled by a mapping function from the instance to the concept. Concept2Box [76] extends JOIE by modeling each concept as a box and by replacing the translation loss with a binary cross entropy loss. EmbedS [22] models each concept as a sphere and each relation by two spheres — one for its domain and the other for its range, and uses distance-based losses to model the RDFS expressions and KG facts. However, EmbedS has not been evaluated.

With the above analysis, we have the following observations and perspectives on embedding ontology with KG.

    •

    All the current methods use geometric modeling, while sequence modeling and graph propagation have not be explored. Through carefully transforming the ontology and the KG into one graph, random walk, sequence learning and GNNs could be applied to learn the embeddings.
    •

    The current works mostly adopt the prediction of concept subsumptions, instance memberships and facts with subsets of DBpedia and Yago for evaluation. We believe that more large-scale benchmarks as well as real-world tasks (such as those in life sciences introduced in Section IV-C) are required to fully evaluate the effectiveness of these methods. Meanwhile, some methods for complex ontology embedding such as Box2​EL [54] can also jointly embed an ontology and a KG, but have not been evaluated and compared with methods in the part.

IV Ontology Embedding Applications

In this section, we first review the works of using ontology embeddings for supporting knowledge engineering and dealing with machine learning sample shortage, and then introduce the applications in life sciences and other domains.
IV-A Knowledge Engineering
IV-A1 Ontology Matching

Given two ontologies 𝒪1 and 𝒪2, ontology matching (OM) (a.k.a. ontology alignment) is to find out entity mappings, i.e., entity pairs in form of (e1,e2), where e1 and e2 come from 𝒪1 and 𝒪2, respectively, with an equivalence or subsumption relationship [100]. Most OM studies aim at the equivalence. A good OM system is expected to have a high Precision for the discovered mappings, a high Recall towards the ground truth mappings, and a high F1 score considering both Precision and Recall. Sometimes, some entities in one of the ontologies are given, and for each of them, an OM system is expected to rank the entities in the other ontology such that the truly matched entity is ranked in the first position. In this situation, ranking-based metrics like MRR (Mean Reciprocal Rank) and Hits@K (K=1, 5, 10, …) are often used to evaluate the performance.

Successful traditional systems such as LogMap [101] mostly use some of the following three techniques: lexical matching with the names, graph structure matching, and logical reasoning for consistency checking and filtering of the mappings. However, these techniques are limited in several aspects such as understanding the text and utilising different semantics. Ontology embeddings provide a promising solution to address these limitations, and thus have been applied to support OM by several recent studies. Meanwhile, those OM benchmarks and settings that are specifically developed for evaluating machine learning-based OM systems (such as Bio-ML [102]) provide a good way for evaluating ontology embeddings.

Most ontology embedding-based OM methods consider literals as due to their important information. The early methods ERSOM [80] and DeepAlignment [82] directly calculate the distance of two concept embeddings, while LogMap-ML [103] and SORBERT [89] further trains a supervised mapping classifier which uses the concept embeddings as the input. Both solutions exploit the embedded semantics for discovering mappings, but the improvement over the traditional systems is still limited as the embeddings are general with no specification to OM. For better performance, most recent OM methods including MEDTO [92], [85], BERTMap [97], BERTSubs [88] and [55] jointly learn task specific embeddings of an ontology with a model for matching. For example, BERTMap [97] fine-tunes a PLM like BERT for encoding concepts with their names, using synonyms from the two ontologies and the given mappings in option, while BERTSubs [88] fine-tunes a PLM for encoding concepts with prompts from their contexts for predicting concept subsumption mappings.
IV-A2 Ontology Reasoning

The embeddings of an ontology can be used to infer its missing knowledge, among which different forms of concept subsumptions such as C⊑D, C⊓D⊑E, C⊑∃r.D and ∃r.D⊑C, concept memberships, property domains and ranges are commonly considered by many current studies (e.g., [54], [68], [67], [26], [88], [19] and [73]). In evaluation for concept subsumption inference, the sub-concept of each subsumption to infer is often given, a set of candidate concepts are ranked according to the score of being the super-concept, and the ground truth super-concept is expected to be ranked in the top position. The ranking-based metrics MRR and Hits@K are often adopted for performance measurement. Note the selection of candidate concepts can be quite flexible, depending on the benchmarking requirement. For example, they can be all the named or complex concepts that exist in the ontology except for the sub-concept itself, a particularly selected subset of them (such as those that are hard to be distinguished from the ground truth super-concept but do not subsume the sub-concept), or some particularly constructed complex concepts. The evaluation for concept membership inference is similar, with an instance given and a set of candidate concepts ranked.

Meanwhile, there are two settings for inference:

    •

    Prediction. A small part of the axioms are splitted out from all the declared axioms of the ontology for testing, and the remaining declared axioms are used for training. The models are expected to capture more generalizable patterns for achieving better prediction performance.
    •

    Approximate Deductive Inference. The declared axioms are used for training, while the axioms entailed by an ontology reasoner are used for testing. This setting is often used for measuring whether the ontology embeddings have retained all the formal semantics.

Embeddings learned by different solutions are applied for ontology reasoning using different paradigms. (i) For embeddings by geometric modeling, axioms can usually be directly inferred by calculating the geometric relationship in the vector space. For the embeddings that represent concepts by boxes (e.g., Box2​EL [54] and BoxEL [68]), C⊑D can be inferred if the box of C is fully inside the box of D. Otherwise, a score can also be calculated according to the relative volume of the overlap. (ii) For embeddings by sequence learning and graph propagation, they usually do not strictly keep the logical relationships but learn symbol correlations and co-occurrences, and thus cannot directly utilise any geometric relationship for inference. Their embedding can be regarded as pre-trained machine learning features and can be fed into another separately trained machine learning models like binary classifiers for concept subsumption prediction (e.g., [26, 84]) and unsupervised clusters for concept clustering (e.g., [104]). Such embeddings sometimes are trained jointly with another model specifically for a downstream task (e.g., in BERTSubs [88], a PLM is fine-tuned for encoding concepts for concept subsumption prediction).
IV-A3 Discussion

Through analysing the current ontology embedding works for knowledge engineering, we have the following observations. (i) Embeddings by geometric modeling can support interpretable knowledge inference, but their performance is usually worse than embeddings by sequence modeling with textual literals incorporated. It is challenging but promising to incorporate semantics of literals in geometric modeling for interpretable knowledge inference with high performance. (ii) Most experiments for evaluation assume a part of the knowledge to infer (e.g., the sub-concept of a concept subsumption axiom) are given, but such settings still require much human support in real-life scenarios. We need to develop more benchmarks and metrics for supporting end-to-end evaluation. (iii) The application of ontology embeddings for knowledge engineering mostly lie in OM and missing knowledge inference, although they can be applied to many other tasks as KG embeddings, including entity resolution, erroneous knowledge detection, query answering, knowledge retrieval, ontology learning from text and so on. Exploring more tasks is one of the most straightforward directions for evaluating ontology embeddings.
IV-B Knowledge Augmented Machine Learning

Ontologies are able to represent information of machine learning tasks, datasets and algorithms, and thus ontology embedding can be a straightforward medium to inject domain knowledge into machine learning training or prediction. One typical aspect for augmentation is dealing with the sample shortage problem [105][106]. In this part, we introduce a case study of using ontology embeddings for zero-shot learning (ZSL) which typically refers to a machine learning classification task7 with some or all of its testing labels unseen in training [107][28]. Given a set of training samples 𝒟t​r={(x,y)|x∈𝒳s,y∈𝒴s}, standard ZSL is to train a model for predicting a set of testing samples 𝒟t​e={(x,y)|x∈𝒳u,y∈𝒴u}, where 𝒳s/𝒳u and 𝒴s/𝒴u denote the sets of inputs and outputs (i.e., classification labels), respectively, and 𝒴s∩𝒴u=∅. 𝒴s and 𝒴u are known as seen labels and unseen labels, respectively. It is possible that 𝒟t​e includes samples of both 𝒴s and 𝒴u, and this setting is called generalized ZSL (GZSL). The model is expected to have high accuracy on testing samples of both seen and unseen labels.

Ontology-aware ZSL requires to construct or re-use an ontology that models the relationships of seen and unseen classification labels, where each label is often represented as a concept in the ontology. For example, in animal image classification, such an ontology could represent animal taxonomies, visual characteristics, habitats, and so on. With the ontology and its embeddings, there are mainly two paradigms among the current studies:

    •

    Mapping-based. In training, this paradigm learns a mapping function from 𝒟t​r to map the vector representation (e.g., image features) of the input x to the ontology embedding of the output label y. In prediction, the mapping function is applied to map the test input into an embedding, and the label (either seen or unseen) whose embedding is closest to this embedding is regarded as the output. It can also map the label’s embedding to the input’s vector, or map both to a common vector. One example work is [108] which uses EL Embedding [23] to embed an ontology of OWL EL for modeling labels of animals for zero-shot animal image classification.
    •

    Generative. This paradigm generates samples for an unseen label according to its embedding in the ontology, by learning a conditional generative model like Generative Adversarial Network (GAN). Thus the ZSL problem is transformed into a normal supervised learning problem. A representative work is OntoZSL [29] which embeds literal-aware ontologies for zero-shot image classification, and zero-shot KG link prediction with unseen relations.

Applying ontology embedding for machine learning sample shortage is a promising solution of neural-symbolic integration, but there is still a shortage of successful systems under deployment. We think the limitation lies in the representation of more complex knowledge such as uncertain relationships (e.g., an image of horse may have a background of grass land, but not always), as well as the automatic construction of the ontology for a specific task. The corresponding solutions could be more flexible multi-modal ontologies with different kinds of literals and example instances, and more tools for knowledge integration and automatic ontology construction.
IV-C Life Sciences

In the realm of life sciences, the development of successful ontologies such as the Gene Ontology [9], SNOMED-CT [42] and the Human Phenotype Ontology [109] has motivated the development of methods that incorporate ontologies as a source of background knowledge. With the rise of machine learning, generating and using ontology embeddings has become a common strategy in order to leverage the ontology. In this part, we review works of two kinds of life science tasks which widely use ontology embeddings: (i) protein-protein interaction, gene-disease association and protein/gene function prediction, and (ii) healtcare predictive analysis with Electronic Health Records (EHR).

Methods for these tasks are usually evaluated as (i) classification systems with metrics such as Precision and Recall, (ii) ranking systems with metrics like MRR and Hits@K, or (iii) predictive systems with metrics like Accuracy@K (K=5,20,…). In particular cases, methods are evaluated on problem-specific metrics. For example, protein function prediction uses Fmax which is obtained from Precision and Recall scores, and Smin which computes the level of uncertainty and misinformation of the predictions.

The central idea of generating ontology embeddings is to capture an entity’s latent relationships with other entities within the ontology. For example, in protein-protein interaction whose objective is to predict if two proteins interact, methods such as [25] and [87] link protein entities to their corresponding functions in the Gene Ontology, and thus the generated embeddings of protein entities encode information about their functions that can be utilised for interaction prediction. A similar idea is followed in the gene-disease association problem. For example, in [25], [119], [120] and [122], genes and disease entities are linked to their corresponding phenotypes in a phenotype ontology, with the goal of capturing phenotypic-related information in the embeddings. Other strategies, such as [118], generate only phenotype embeddings and then compute an embedding for a gene (or disease) using their associated phenotype embeddings. In protein function prediction, embeddings for functions are obtained from the Gene Ontology, whereas embeddings for proteins are obtained from protein sequences, where the use of Protein Language Models [128][129][130] is currently predominant.

In healthcare, there are various EHR predictive analysis tasks such as mortality prediction, next-admission diagnosis prediction or hospital readmission prediction where hierarchical medical concepts are exploited: an ontology is compiled into a directed acyclic graph and its embedding is combined with the textual information retrieved from EHRs. In most of the works including [110], [111], [112], [116], [113], [114], [115] and [121], the graph-based attention mechanism is applied to leverage hierarchical structure of an ontology; another approach is described in [62] and [121] where concept hierarchies are embeded through hyperbolic embedding. Embedded medical concepts can then be used for training a Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN) for sequential diagnosis prediction or mortality prediction. [117] uses a dual RNN with co-attention and max pooling to fuse medical term hierarchies of patient diagnoses and drugs for prescription recommendation. Several approaches use embeddings from multiple ontologies or multiple representations of a single ontology: [113] combines categorical and multi-relational ontologies via a category-aware graph attention network for multi-relational ontology embedding, and [115] assigns multiple embeddings for non-leaf nodes (except for the root node) of the ontology’s directed acyclic graph.

The strategies that have been employed to utilise ontology embeddings for life science can be summarised as follows:

    •

    Similarity-based strategy: Given the trained embeddings of a pair of entities, this strategy computes the pair’s score by either directly calling a similarity function or using a neural network that is additionally trained for prediction. One embedding method that is often applied in this strategy is OPA2Vec [25], which is used for predicting protein-protein interactions and gene-disease associations. OPA2Vec generates a corpus from ontology axioms and textual descriptions of ontology entities, and then trains a sequence learning model like Word2Vec from the corpus for the embeddings. More works that adopt this strategy include [118], [119] and [120]. Their embeddings are also based on the sequence learning model Word2Vec, trained with random walks of the graph extracted from the ontology.
    •

    Graph-based strategy: This strategy is usually based on a graph created from one or many ontologies, applying techniques of KG embedding (KGE), GNN, RNN, hyperbolic embedding, graph-based attention, and so on. [122], [123] and [87] frame the problem as link prediction on the graph, and address it by KGE methods such as TransE [15], DistMult [47] and ComplEx [131]. OntoProtein [87] uses the graph to enhance the training of a Protein Language Model for better protein embedding and feeds the embedding into an additional neural network for prediction. PO2GO [127] embeds a directed acyclic graph from the Gene Ontology using a partial-order embedding method and also adopts an additional neural network for prediction. Works adopting GNNs either frame the prediction problem as node classification [124] or use embeddings as partial input of a prediction module [125]. Graph-based attention models follow the original GRAM method [110] where the final embeddings of leaf nodes are convex combinations of their own embeddings and their ancestors’ embeddings. More works include learning interaction of hierarchical embeddings of drug and diagnoses concepts by a dual RNN co-attention model [117] and utilizing the Poincaré ball model [62][121].
    •

    Model-theoretic strategy: Since OWL ontologies are formal semantics, rooted in Description Logics, this strategy aims to utilise embeddings that are generated for theories. In this case, the prediction problem is framed as the inference of axioms with the embeddings. Methods such as [28] for protein-function prediction and variations of [120] for gene-disease associations employ a model-theoretic embedding method ELEmbeddings [23]. [126] further generates multiple embedding models for approximate semantic entailment.

IV-D Other Applications

Ontology utilization in other domains is not as prevalent as in life science applications; one possible reason for that is the absence of well-curated and widely used ontologies developed for specific domain knowledge. Some examples of ontology exploitation with embedding include ontology-aware classifiers built for identifying research topics in scholarly articles [132], enhancing intelligent transportation systems [133], sentiment analysis [134], event detection [135] and company cointegration prediction [136]. In all these works, ontologies used within learning frameworks were developed from scratch. Ontology embedding strategies in these selected studies are mostly quite simple or straightforward by applying some word or KG embedding methods, including Word2Vec in [132] and [133], XLNet for aspect-based sentiment extraction in [134], IterE [98] and Node2Vec [46] applied to ontology with KG in [135] and [136]. The work [137] discusses the task-dependent and task-independent evaluation of Poincaré disk ontology embeddings for the GeoNames [138] ontology.
V mOWL: A Machine learning library with Ontology Embedding Methods

Many ontology embedding works release the implementations of their methods, but there is a shortage of easy-to-use softwares that have implemented multiple ontology embedding methods and can support the implementation of new methods. mOWL8 [39], a library that provides functions to manipulate ontologies and use ontologies with machine learning, aims to bridge this gap. It provides Python interfaces and can support the following functions: (i) ontology manipulation, where the OWL API [139] is accessed for ontology creation, manipulation and reasoning; (ii) ontology transformation, which enables extracting graphs (such as the class hierarchy) or corpora (such as the sentences used for training OWL2Vec*) from ontologies; (iii) implementation of ontology embedding methods that aim at semantics of DL ℰ​ℒ+⁣+.

mOWL modules abstract different components of many ontology embedding methods described in Section III-A. It has several workflows, following the modular design patterns for neural-symbolic systems in [140]. All the workflows use ontologies as input and output embeddings. VI Challenges and Future Directions

Although many studies have been done for ontology embedding, this is still a relatively new direction, and there are several challenges that prevent ontology embedding from more real-world applications. We believe more future works are required in at least the following aspects.

Efficient Geometric Modeling for Complex Ontologies. On the one hand, quite a few geometric modeling methods in the Euclidean space have been proposed, but they are mostly limited to the main features (constructs) of Description Logic fragments of ℰ​ℒ+⁣+ and 𝒜​ℒ​𝒞. Many other features like the at-least and at-most restrictions have not been explored, not alone modeling the complete formal semantics of an arbitrary OWL ontology. Thus we need to extend these methods to support more features with faithful embeddings, especially those that are commonly used in real-world ontologies. On the other hand, the main contents of real-world ontologies are usually the hierarchical concepts, but their modeling in the Euclidean space (e.g., by high dimensional boxes) is much less efficient (i.e., requires many more parameters to learn) than their modeling in some hyperbolic spaces such as Poincaré ball in which the distance of points increases exponentially as they get closer to the boundary [19]. There have been several studies to explore geometric modeling in hyperbolic spaces for ontology embedding, such as [19], [91], [61] and [64], but they mostly can only embed the hierarchical concepts. Geometric modeling in hyperbolic spaces for not only hierarchical concepts but also some other ontology features deserves higher attention.

Utilising and Supporting Large Language Models. Large Language Models (LLMs) like the GPT series by OpenAI and the Llama series by Meta have shown great success in understanding not only natural language but also images, videos, and (semi-)structured data [141][142]. A straightforward but promising idea is to embed ontologies, especially those with literals, using LLMs. There have been some ontology embedding methods that use encoder-based language models, such as OWL2Vec∗ [26] and OPA2Vec [25] which use Word2Vec, and HiT [90] which uses BERT alike Transformer encoder-based models, but the LLMs for generation are quite different as they adopt some decoder or encoder-decoder architectures. Therefore, novel solutions, such as further pre-training with OWL ontologies and instruction turning for the adaption to OWL ontologies, need to be explored to apply them for ontology embedding. Meanwhile, LLMs also suffer from many problems including hallucination, black-box, short of domain knowledge and so on. Integrating KGs as well as other (semi-)structured data is widely regarded as a promising solution [143][50]. How can we use ontology embedding to integrate ontologies with LLMs? How to support Retrieval Augmented Generation (RAG) [144] with ontology embedding for incorporating domain knowledge in reasoning? Both questions are quite interesting for the future work of ontology embedding.

Application in Neural-symbolic Integration and other Domains. Currently, ontology embedding is mostly applied to construct and curate ontologies themselves, and link prediction for domains like life sciences where ontologies have been widely applied to represent data and knowledge. As a popular semantic technique, ontology has a high potential for building neural-symbolic integration [145], and one promising direction is to utilise ontology embedding for injecting domain knowledge into machine learning. Although some works have been proposed for ontology-based zero-shot and few-shot learning [105], we believe the research of this direction is far from enough, with many topics like using ontologies for supporting meta learning (e.g., model selection) and augmenting explanation, not fully explored. Meanwhile, more domain applications, both in and out of life sciences, should be considered for exploring the potential of ontology embeddings. The current life science applications, which only consider link prediction of several simple relations and use formal semantics without literals, are quite simple. Link prediction with more complex target relations and data of different modalities can be explored using literal-aware ontology embedding methods. Other tasks, such as generation for drug discovery and protein design [146] and natural language inference for clinical trial [147], can also be explored with ontology embedding.

Benchmarking. This direction still lacks systematic benchmarking resources. Ontology construction and inference are straightforward and effective for evaluating different aspects of ontology embeddings, including how much formal and informal semantics the embeddings retain. But most of the current works limit the evaluation to concept subsumption inference and concept alignment. More complex tasks in ontology learning, either utilizing or not utilizing external resources, such as learning complex concepts, inferring complex axioms and inserting new concepts, have been rarely considered. Meanwhile, these aforementioned tasks for neural-symbolic integration and domain applications can be considered as real-world benchmarks.
VII Conclusion

This is a comprehensive survey of the raising direction ontology embedding which is to represent knowledge of ontologies in vector spaces with their semantics (partially) retained. It summarises three different technical solutions, i.e., geometric modeling, sequence modeling and graph propagation, and categorises the studies according to not only the methods used but also the ontologies they aim at (including simple ontology, complex ontology in OWL or RDFS, ontology with literal and ontology with KG). Following the method part, the survey also gives a relatively complete analysis for the application of ontology embedding in knowledge engineering, life sciences and machine learning augmentation, and demonstrates a library mOWL developed by the co-authors that has implemented several typical ontology embedding methods and benchmarking resources. In the end, the survey discusses some potential future directions, including the interesting topics of integrating ontology embedding with LLMs.
